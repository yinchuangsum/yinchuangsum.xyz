Last Friday, Wendy invited me to join a panel she hosted at the Malaysian AI Show & Tell. The topic: balancing full-time work and startup validation. The room was full of aspiring founders and engineers who were trying to figure out the same puzzle I've been solving for years — how do you build something of your own while keeping the lights on?

I walked away from that conversation with a clearer picture of my own path. This is that story, as I told it on stage.

## My Journey

I didn't start as an engineer. Fresh out of university, I was a photographer. And for a stretch after graduating, I had zero income. That period planted a deep financial insecurity that took years to shake — it also taught me what the bottom actually looks like.

When I decided to pivot, I went all in. Six months of self-taught engineering, then I landed a junior role at an MNC. The real work started after 5 PM — I'd clock out from my 9-to-5 and clock into a second shift from 6 PM to midnight, building side projects. I kept that rhythm for two to three years.

That grind eventually led to co-founding PigeonBook, where I'm now full-time.

The most honest thing I can tell you about that arc: being outstanding isn't about being loud. It's about stepping out of your comfort zone, joining communities, and letting people see you show up, consistently. The opportunities that changed my trajectory came when more senior folks noticed my work ethic and extended a hand.

## Advice I'd Give

The panel Q&A pulled out the things I wish someone had told me earlier.

**On co-founders.** My biggest mistake was partnering with someone whose strengths mirrored mine — another tech person. What you actually want is complementary skills. A technical co-founder needs a business-oriented counterpart, and vice versa. Same-shape co-founders leave gaps that neither of you can fill.

**On impostor syndrome.** I've hit rock bottom before. Losing a job. Photography income drying up to nothing. When you've been at zero, everything above it feels like ground you can stand on. I've come to see pressure as a signal — not that you're failing, but that you're doing something hard and worthwhile. If there's no pressure, you're probably not growing.

**On networking.** I described it as speed dating. Some conversations click, most don't. Getting ignored is normal, not personal. For the conversations that actually matter — investors, key partners, senior mentors — the real work happens before you ever speak. Build a reputation first, then the conversation becomes a formality.

**On the two doors approach.** Keep your day job while you build your side business. Don't quit until someone has actually paid you for what you're building. Wendy articulated this cleanly — Door 1 is your full-time income, Door 2 is your startup. Walk through Door 2 only after you've validated the business model with real money from real strangers.
